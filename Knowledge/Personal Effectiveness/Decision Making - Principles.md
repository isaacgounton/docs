# Decision Making - Principles

> Seven mental models for better decisions: first principles, opportunity cost, second-order thinking, compounding, incentives, probabilistic thinking, and inversion, each with a guiding question.

---

## Overview

The source argues that better decisions come less from more information and more from a better way of thinking: two people with the same facts decide differently because they apply different mental models. It presents seven well-known models, framed for founders, creators, and early-career builders. None of the models is new; the value is having them as a compact checklist.

---

## The Seven Models

| # | Model | Guiding question | Typical failure it prevents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First principles | "What is fundamentally true here?" | Copying how everyone else does it |
| 2 | Opportunity cost | "What does this yes say no to?" | Judging a choice in isolation |
| 3 | Second-order thinking | "And then what happens?" | Optimising for the first visible outcome |
| 4 | Compounding | "What are my daily choices compounding into?" | Quitting before repetition pays off |
| 5 | Incentives | "What does this person gain if I believe them?" | Taking advice at face value |
| 6 | Probabilistic thinking | "Does this raise my odds of success?" | Waiting for certainty that never comes |
| 7 | Inversion | "What would guarantee failure?" | Missing obvious avoidable mistakes |

### 1. First Principles Thinking

Break a problem into its basic truths and build up from there instead of inheriting assumptions. Examples from the source: a founder may not need investors first, but paying customers; a company may not need an office, but a laptop and internet connection. Questioning "do they really need X?" surfaces options most people never consider.

### 2. Opportunity Cost

Every decision has a hidden price: what it replaces. Three hours of scrolling is three hours not spent writing, building, or meeting someone; buying a phone is money not invested. Think in trade-offs, not purchases. The point is intentionality, not guilt about rest.

### 3. Second-Order Thinking

Look one or two steps past the immediate effect. Skipping a conference saves money (first order) but may cost the meeting with a future client, investor, or co-founder (second order). Many life-changing decisions look small at the time; favour choices that create the most future opportunities, not the biggest immediate reward.

### 4. Compounding

Single actions (one workout, one article, one conversation) rarely matter; hundreds of repetitions do. People quit because they expect immediate results from things that only pay off at scale. It works in both directions: small bad habits compound too.

### 5. Incentives

Judge advice by what the adviser gains, not only by what they say. Influencers, VC-backed founders, employers pushing office work, and "quit your job" YouTubers all have goals shaping their recommendations. This does not mean they are lying; it means filter accordingly.

### 6. Probabilistic Thinking

Certainty almost never arrives. Investors, founders, and creators act anyway. Replace "Will this work?" with "Does this increase my probability of succeeding?" Winners are not better predictors; they consistently place better bets.

### 7. Inversion

Think backward from failure. Behaviours that almost guarantee it: never publishing, never talking to users, never taking risks, never asking questions, never starting. Avoiding those already puts you ahead of most people.

> [!tip]
> Before a significant decision, run the table as a checklist: state the fundamental truth, name the opportunity cost, trace one second-order effect, check the adviser's incentive, and ask what would make it fail. Five questions, five minutes.

> [!note]
> Several models reinforce each other: compounding explains why second-order effects of small habits are large, and probabilistic thinking plus inversion together suggest "take many cheap bets, avoid the ruinous ones".
